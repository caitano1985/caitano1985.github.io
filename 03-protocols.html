Trading Protocols & Connectivity

How systems that have never met agree on what a message means, how it travels, how loss is detected, and how a stream is rebuilt after something goes missing.

Journal 02 — The Order Lifecycle

In the previous entry we followed one order from a human decision to a confirmation on a screen. We treated the message as if it simply arrived. This chapter is about why it arrives at all — and what happens when it does not.

A protocol is not a message format. That is the part everyone learns and the part that matters least. A protocol is the complete set of rules for how two systems that have never met establish a relationship, identify themselves, agree on what a field means, confirm receipt, notice that something is missing, and repair the stream without stopping it.

WHERE YOUR DISCIPLINE STOPS BEING NETWORKING

Up to this point a network engineer could stay inside familiar territory: addresses, ports, paths, loss, latency. From here the work sits at an intersection — application engineering, network engineering, market infrastructure and reliability engineering, all at once. An engineer who can only reason about one of those four will diagnose confidently and be wrong.

I

Protocol is not transport

Before FIX, before TCP, before multicast, separate the layers. Each one answers a different question, and mixing them produces the kind of sentence that ends a technical conversation: "the FIX is slow". FIX has no speed. It is a grammar.

WHAT DOES THIS MESSAGE MEAN?

The business event itself. A human intention that has been turned into something a machine can act on. At this layer there is no protocol, no port and no packet — only a decision with a price on it.

FIX does not replace TCP. FIX rides on TCP. One decides what a field means; the other decides whether the bytes arrive.

II

Message is not segment is not packet is not frame

This distinction is the reason packet capture is a skill and not a button. Open a capture of a trading session and you will not find the string "BUY 100 XYZ". You will find headers, ports, flags, sequence numbers and a payload — and the work is rebuilding the business event from them.

III

A protocol solves a language problem

Two institutions that have never spoken need to agree, in advance and in writing, on what every field means. FIX — the Financial Information eXchange — is the best known of these agreements, and it carries orders, executions, cancellations, amendments and session events.

But be careful with the trap that follows: knowing FIX is not knowing the market. Venues run binary protocols, proprietary protocols, APIs and mechanisms specific to each house. When microseconds matter, the encoding itself becomes an engineering decision.

TEXT ENCODING

35=D|55=XYZ|54=1|
38=100|44=100.50|
40=2|11=ORD0001

Human-readable, self-describing, easy to debug with your eyes. Costs more bytes and a parse step that is variable in length — and variable length means variable time.

BINARY ENCODING

[msg type   ] 1B
[instrument ] 4B
[side       ] 1B
[quantity   ] 4B
[price      ] 8B
[sequence   ] 4B

Fixed offsets, fixed size, no parsing ambiguity. Reading field four is arithmetic, not searching. The gain is not bytes saved — it is predictability.

WHY PREDICTABILITY BEATS SIZE

People justify binary protocols with bandwidth. That is the weakest argument. The real reason is that a fixed-layout message parses in constant time, with no branching on content and no allocation — which means the p99.9 of your parse step looks like the median. In a system judged on its tail, an encoding that removes variance is worth more than one that removes bytes.

IV

TCP or UDP is the wrong question

"UDP is fast, TCP is slow" is the sentence that identifies someone who has not built either. Both move bits at the same speed. What differs is which guarantees the transport provides and which ones your application must build itself.

The correct question is never which protocol is better. It is: which property does this system actually need? Pick a requirement below.

WHICH PROPERTY DOES THE SYSTEM NEED?
TCP

Silent loss of an order is unacceptable, so the transport must acknowledge and retransmit. You pay for that with connection state and with a retransmission delay you do not control. For order flow, that trade is correct — a late order is bad, a lost order is catastrophic.

V

Multicast turns distribution into publish and subscribe

Now the market data side. The matching engine produces book changes, trades, quote updates — and thousands of participants need all of them, at the same instant, with no participant advantaged by the order in which the exchange happens to send.

Sending one copy per participant is not only expensive; it is unfair. The last subscriber on the list receives later than the first. Multicast removes that problem by removing the list.

DISTRIBUTION

Unicast replication versus multicast publication

Fairness is the reason, not efficiency. With multicast the exchange publishes once and the network delivers in parallel — no participant is structurally last.

WHAT YOU JUST GAVE UP

Multicast is UDP, and UDP will drop. The exchange publishes once and never asks whether you received it. You have solved fairness and fan-out, and in exchange you now own a problem that TCP used to own for you: knowing that something is missing. That problem is the rest of this chapter.

VI

The sequence number, the gap, and the repair

Here is the mechanism that makes an unreliable transport carry a market. Every message on the feed carries a sequence number. The receiver holds one piece of state — the number it expects next — and compares. That is the whole idea, and it is enough.

THE PATTERN THAT REPEATS EVERYWHERE

Sequence, then detection, then recovery, then consistency. You will meet this pattern again in session protocols, in replication, in event sourcing and in every distributed system that must not lose an event. Learn it here, on a market data feed, where it is at its most visible — and where the cost of getting it wrong is a book that silently disagrees with the exchange.

Notice what the recovery channel is not: it is not the live feed. Mixing repair traffic into the real-time path is how a small loss becomes a large outage, because the burst of retransmission arrives exactly when the network is already struggling. Recovery belongs on its own path, with its own capacity.

VII

Two feeds, because one path is a single point of failure

Detection and recovery handle occasional loss. They do not handle a degraded path, because a path that is dropping steadily will keep you permanently in repair — always behind, always requesting, never current.

So exchanges publish the same stream twice, over independent paths, and the receiver arbitrates: take whichever copy of each sequence arrives first, discard the duplicate. Run it.

A/B ARBITRATION

FEED B · path 2

IDLE

Both feeds carry the same sequence. Each will lose different messages.

THE MONITORING CONSEQUENCE

A/B arbitration is dangerous precisely because it works. A feed can degrade for weeks without anyone noticing, because the other feed is quietly covering for it — until the day the healthy one has a problem too and both fail at once. Monitor each feed's loss rate independently, before arbitration. The arbitrated output is the wrong place to measure health; it is designed to look healthy.

VIII

Order flow needs a relationship, not just a connection

Market data is anonymous broadcast. Order flow is the opposite: the venue must know who you are, which messages it has already accepted from you, and whether you are still there. That is a session.

Click through the states. Pay attention to the one in the middle — the heartbeat is not a formality.

SESSION

From connection to logout, and the failure branch

LOGON

The participant identifies itself and both sides agree on where the message sequence stands. This is also where a mismatch surfaces: if your side thinks it sent 4,812 messages and the venue counted 4,809, the disagreement appears here, before any business message flows.

The heartbeat exists because silence is ambiguous. A quiet session might mean a calm market or a dead link, and those require opposite responses. Without a heartbeat you discover the link is gone at the worst possible moment: when you finally have an order to send.

In a critical environment, how fast you detect a failure is part of the engineering — not an operational detail to be tuned later.

INCIDENT CLOSED

The symptom was a gap and the instinct was to blame the network — and the network was clean. What this lab really tested is whether you stop at the first plausible cause. Every layer between the wire and the application can drop a message, and only one of them belongs to the network team. An engineer who can prove where loss occurred, with timestamps, is worth more in an incident than one who can only report that it did.

X

What changes after this chapter

You now have the vocabulary to say precisely where something happened. Not "the feed had a problem" — but "sequence 501200 was on the wire at 10:14:22.0078 and was discarded at the socket buffer". That sentence is worth a career.

The next descent is the one that makes this chapter actionable. We have been saying "the application did not read fast enough" as if it were a single event. It is not. Between the bits on fibre and the line of code that sees the message there is an entire machine: NIC, PCIe, kernel, socket, interrupt or poll, CPU, cache. That path has its own latency, its own variance and its own failure modes — and it is where low-latency engineering actually lives.

Reliability did not disappear when we chose UDP. It moved — into sequence numbers, into recovery channels, into a second feed, and into the engineer who knows how to prove where a message died.
