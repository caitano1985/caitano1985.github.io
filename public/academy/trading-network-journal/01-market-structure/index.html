Trading Network Journal · 01

Market Structure

Before a single packet is engineered, we need to agree on what that packet is carrying. This is the market mechanism that every piece of trading infrastructure exists to execute.

JC
Josimar Caitano
Principal Solutions Architect · NTT DATA · Capital Markets Infrastructure
September 2026 25 min · with lab Market Structure · Foundation

I spent years designing networks for financial institutions before I could answer a simple question properly: what is actually inside the packet I am optimizing? Not because the question is difficult. Because nobody asks it. The engineer is handed a latency budget and a topology, and the business logic above the wire remains someone else's problem.

That gap is where careers stall. An engineer who does not know what a matching engine does cannot write a meaningful SLO for it. An architect who does not distinguish order flow from market data will design both with the same tools and fail at both. So we start here, at the business mechanism, and only then descend into the infrastructure.

HOW TO USE THIS JOURNAL

This is the first entry of a sequence. Each entry descends one layer: from market mechanism, to order lifecycle, to protocols, to timestamps, to observability. Every entry closes with a lab you are expected to solve before moving on. If you skip the lab, you have read the chapter — you have not learned it.

I

The chain nobody draws

The mental model most engineers carry is two nodes: trader sends order, exchange executes. That model is not simplified — it is wrong, and architecture built on it inherits the error. There is an ordered chain, and each link imposes a different constraint on the infrastructure beneath it.

PARTICIPANT CHAIN

From intention to settled trade

Five links, three protocols, two time domains. The exchange band operates in microseconds; the post-trade band operates in hours. Same chain, different physics.

In Brazil, B3 occupies the exchange band for equities, derivatives and fixed income. The Listados segment — the one I care about most in this journal — covers exactly two of these links simultaneously: Negociação and Pós-Negociação. Trading and post-trade. One system, two sets of physics.

II

Trading and post-trade are not the same system

This is the boundary I want you to internalize before anything else. Merging these two into a single "trading infrastructure project" is the most common structural mistake I have seen inside financial institutions — and it produces mispriced risk in both directions.

TRADING

BUY 100 WIN
     ↓
   ORDER
     ↓
  MATCHING
     ↓
   TRADE

Microseconds. Determinism. Zero tolerance for jitter on the order path. Latency is not a performance metric here — it decides who wins the trade.

POST-TRADE

TRADE
   ↓
CLEARING
   ↓
  RISK
   ↓
SETTLEMENT
   ↓
 CUSTODY

Hours to days. Reliability over speed. Reconciliation, auditability, recovery. This is where SRE and resilience become the dominant discipline.

WHY THIS MATTERS FOR SLO DESIGN

A 99.99% availability target means something entirely different on each side. On the trading side, four nines with a 200 ms tail is a failed system — the tail is where the money is lost. On the post-trade side, a brief outage that recovers with complete reconciliation may be operationally acceptable. Same number, opposite meaning. An SLO written without that distinction is decoration.

III

Four worlds, four sets of constraints

The ecosystem divides cleanly into four domains. Know which one you are designing for before you open a terminal — the same technology decision is correct in one and catastrophic in another.

01 · BUY SIDE

Who invests

Asset managers, hedge funds, proprietary trading firms, pension funds. Their infrastructure exists to generate and capture alpha. Execution quality is the metric.

02 · SELL SIDE

Who provides access

Investment banks, brokers, dealers, market makers. They own order routing, pre-trade risk and DMA. Their constraint is throughput under regulatory obligation.

03 · EXCHANGE

Where orders meet

Matching engine, order gateway, market data feed, colocation. Deterministic latency is not a goal — it is the fairness contract with the entire market.

04 · POST-TRADE

After the trade

Clearing, risk, settlement, custody, reconciliation. Consistency and recoverability dominate. This is the natural home of SRE practice in capital markets.

Two institutions can be equally compliant with the same exchange manual and operate three orders of magnitude apart. Compliance is the floor, not the strategy.

IV

An order is not a trade

This is the first thing I ask any engineer joining a market infrastructure team, and it filters more candidates than any protocol question. An order is an instruction. A trade is an event produced when two compatible instructions meet. They are different objects with different lifecycles, different volumes and different failure modes.

An order can rest on the book indefinitely. It can be modified. It can be cancelled. It can be partially filled. Only when the matching engine finds a compatible counterparty does a trade exist.

THE NUMBER THAT BREAKS CAPACITY MODELS

In active markets the order-to-trade ratio routinely exceeds 100:1. Every rejected order, every cancellation, every modification still traverses the gateway, consumes bandwidth and demands processing. A capacity model built on executed trade volume underprovisions the system by two orders of magnitude — and it fails precisely on the days that matter, when volume spikes and the ratio widens.

V

Price-time priority, and why it is an engineering requirement

The order book is the live state of supply and demand at every price level. The matching engine applies one deterministic rule to it: best price first; within the same price, earliest arrival first.

ORDER BOOK · SAMPLE STATE

105.05300
105.00200
spread · 0.05
104.95150
104.90400
104.85800
ASK · sell
BID · buy

Two participants send an identical buy order at the same price. The one whose packet reaches the matching engine first wins the fill. The other gets nothing, or gets a worse price on the next level.

That is the entire argument for everything we will build in the following entries. Timestamp precision, deterministic switching, kernel bypass, colocation — none of it is performance tuning. It is the infrastructure consequence of a matching rule.

  • Hardware timestamping and PTP — you cannot prove event order without a traceable clock
  • Sequence numbers on every message — gap detection is mandatory, not optional
  • No jitter on the order path — an indeterminate delay violates the fairness contract
  • Physical proximity — distance is the one component engineering cannot compensate for
LATENCY RACE

Same order, same price, different path

The 10 µs difference is not a performance improvement. At the same price level it is the entire outcome.

VI

The second flow, running the other way

Order flow carries intention into the market. Market data carries state out of it, to every participant simultaneously. Opposite directions, opposite protocols, opposite failure modes — and engineers who treat them as one system fail at both.

ORDER FLOW · trader → exchange

Protocol   TCP
Topology   unicast 1:1
Volume     moderate
Failure    session drop
Tolerance  zero loss

Silent loss of an order is unacceptable, so the protocol must acknowledge. The cost is connection state.

MARKET DATA · exchange → all

Protocol   UDP multicast
Topology   1:N simultaneous
Volume     enormous
Failure    packet gap
Tolerance  gap + recover

The exchange publishes once; every subscriber receives in parallel. TCP here would collapse under fan-out.

WHY TRADING SERVERS USE DUAL-PORT NICS

This asymmetry has a physical consequence I built with my own hands inside a colocation cage: one NIC port dedicated to multicast receive, a separate port for TCP order transmit. Two opposing requirements on the same machine, separated at the hardware level. Sharing a single port introduces cross-traffic jitter on the order path — and the order path is the one that cannot have it.

VII

The map we carry from here

Everything above is one half of the picture: the business mechanism. Below it sits the stack that executes it. Each layer answers a different question, and each subsequent entry in this journal descends one level.

STACK

From business event down to physical medium

The SRE layer sits exactly where business guarantee meets physical constraint. That position is why the discipline is different in capital markets than anywhere else.

Order A filled 100 at 100.90. Order B swept two levels: 100 at 100.90, then 150 at 100.95. Order C crossed the spread and filled 150 at 100.85, consuming that level entirely. One order produced two trade events — your capacity model must account for that fan-out at every layer.

WHAT THIS LAB ACTUALLY TESTED

Not arithmetic. It tested whether you understand that a limit price is a boundary, not an instruction — a sell at 100.80 executes at 100.85 because 100.85 is better for the seller. Engineers who miss this build validation logic that rejects valid orders. It also tested whether you noticed that one incoming order can produce multiple trade events, which is the single most underestimated variable in market data capacity planning.

VIII

What changes after this chapter

You now have the mechanism. From here, every technical decision in this journal traces back to something in this chapter, and I will make that link explicit every time.

Multicast exists because the exchange must publish once to N subscribers. Sequence numbers exist because UDP will drop and you must prove nothing was lost. PTP exists because price-time priority requires provable event order. Colocation exists because distance is the one cost engineering cannot remove. None of these are technology choices. They are consequences of a market rule.

The packet matters because of what sits above it. Engineer the wire without knowing the rule, and you will optimize the wrong layer with great precision.
