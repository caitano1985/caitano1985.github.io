The Order Lifecycle

One single order, followed end to end. Where it becomes a message, where it becomes a packet, where the clock starts, and every point at which it can quietly die.

Journal 01 — Market Structure

In the previous entry we established that an order is not a trade. That distinction is useless until you can say exactly where, along the path, an order stops being one thing and becomes another. This chapter is that path.

I am going to take a single order and follow it from a human decision to a confirmation arriving back on a screen. Along the way it changes form four times: intention, message, packet, event. Each transformation happens in a different system, costs a different amount of time, and fails in a different way.

WHY THIS CHAPTER DECIDES YOUR DIAGNOSTIC ABILITY

When a trader says "my order did not execute", that sentence has at least seven possible causes, and only one of them involves the matching engine. An engineer who has not internalized the lifecycle will start troubleshooting at the wrong layer under pressure — and in this environment, troubleshooting at the wrong layer is indistinguishable from having no answer at all.

I

Intent is not an order

A trader decides to buy 1,000 contracts at 100.50. That decision is an intention — it exists in a human head and nowhere else. Electronic markets do not process intentions. They process structured messages with mandatory fields, and the trading application is what performs that translation.

This matters more than it sounds. Every field in that message is a decision, and several of them carry latency and risk consequences. Click through them.

Market or limit: the field that changes everything

MARKET ORDER

BUY 1000 XYZ
MARKET

Prioritises execution over price. It will consume whatever liquidity exists, sweeping levels until filled. Certainty of fill, uncertainty of price.

LIMIT ORDER

BUY 1000 XYZ
LIMIT 100.50

Sets a boundary: pay up to 100.50, never above. Certainty of price, uncertainty of fill. It may rest on the book indefinitely.

II

Message is not packet

This is the boundary where my own discipline begins. The application produces an order message. The network transports packets. They are different abstractions of the same flow, observed from different layers, and confusing them makes every latency conversation incoherent.

ABSTRACTION CROSSING

Where an order message becomes wire traffic

An order that is "missing" in the application log may be perfectly visible in a packet capture. The two observations answer different questions.

IV

"Latency" is not a number

This is the question I use to separate engineers who have worked in this environment from those who have read about it. When a vendor or a colleague says "our latency is 8 microseconds", the only correct response is: between what and what?

Pick any two timestamps from the trace above and the answer changes completely — along with the team responsible for it.

LATENCY BETWEEN WHAT AND WHAT?
→
2.5 µs
Application latency
Time inside the trading application, from decision to handing the message to the network stack. Owned by the application team, not the network team.

THE ORGANISATIONAL CONSEQUENCE

Each of those segments has a different owner. Application latency belongs to developers. Wire latency belongs to the network team. Exchange processing belongs to the venue and is not negotiable. When an institution reports a single end-to-end number to its board, it destroys exactly the information needed to decide where investment produces return.

V

Accepted is not executed

An order has an observable lifecycle with discrete states. The exact names depend on protocol and venue, but the shape is universal — and the transitions are what your monitoring must expose. Click any state.

STATE MACHINE

Where is my order right now?

ACCEPTED

The venue confirmed the message is valid and the order now exists in its system. This is the state most often mistaken for execution. Accepted means the order is alive — it says nothing about whether a counterparty was found.

An order can die at six different points before the matching engine ever sees it. Only one of those is a matching engine problem.

VI

WHY THE TAIL IS ASYMMETRIC AGAINST YOU

Tail events are not random with respect to market conditions. They cluster during volatility, message bursts and open/close windows — exactly when opportunity is largest and the cost of missing a fill is highest. Reporting average latency to a risk committee is equivalent to reporting average return with no standard deviation.

LAB 02

Diagnose the order

Three incidents. In each, a trader reports the same symptom: the order did not execute. You have the evidence available to an SRE on call. Identify where in the lifecycle the order actually stopped. Getting this wrong under pressure is how outages get escalated to the wrong team.

LAB 02

Incident triage · lifecycle reasoning

Same symptom, three different root causes, three different owners

INCIDENT 01
Trader: "I sent it at 09:31:04, nothing happened." App log → 09:31:04.118 ORDER SENT ClOrdID 84721 Capture → 09:31:04.118 TCP out, 1 packet, seq ok Gateway → 09:31:04.126 session active, no message received Book → no entry for ClOrdID 84721
Where did the order stop?
AMatching engine — no counterparty was available at that price
BGateway validation — the order was rejected by a risk rule
CConnectivity — the packet left the NIC but never arrived at the gateway
DApplication — the order was never actually constructed
INCIDENT 02
Trader: "It says accepted but I got nothing." Gateway → 09:45:12.004 ACCEPTED ClOrdID 91883 Book → 09:45:12.007 RESTING BUY 500 @ 98.00 Market → best ASK all session: 98.35 Report → no execution report sent
Where did the order stop?
AGateway — the acceptance message was a false positive
BNowhere — the order is healthy and resting; no counterparty met its limit price
CMarket data — the execution happened but the report was lost in transit
DMatching engine — price-time priority was applied incorrectly
INCIDENT 03
Trader: "Half of it filled, the rest disappeared." Gateway → 10:02:55.201 ACCEPTED ClOrdID 77310 BUY 1000 @ 100.50 Engine → 10:02:55.203 FILL 400 @ 100.50 (level exhausted) Book → 10:02:55.203 RESTING 600 @ 100.50 Reports → FILL 400 delivered · no further message Market → ASK moved to 100.55 and stayed
What is the correct state of the remaining 600?
ACancelled — a partial fill automatically cancels the remainder
BLost — the engine dropped the balance when the level was exhausted
CPartially filled and still open — 600 rest at 100.50, waiting for the ASK to come back down
DRejected — the engine rejected the balance because the price moved
TRIAGE COMPLETE

Three identical complaints, three different owners: network, nobody, and the trader's own expectation. Notice that in two of the three cases the correct action is not to fix anything — it is to explain the lifecycle to the person reporting the incident. That conversation is only possible for an engineer who can locate the order precisely.

VII

The architecture to memorise

If you keep one diagram from this chapter, keep this one. Everything in the following entries attaches to a point on it.

FULL LIFECYCLE

From intent to post-trade, with both return flows

One order produces up to three outbound flows from a single match: a private report to the participant, a public update to every subscriber, and a record to clearing.

That fan-out is the detail most capacity models omit. A single incoming order can generate multiple trade events, each of which generates a report and a market data update. The output volume is not proportional to the input volume — it is a multiple of it, and the multiplier rises with volatility.

You cannot observe what you cannot locate. The lifecycle is not documentation — it is the coordinate system your telemetry needs.
