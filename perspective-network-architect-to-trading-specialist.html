Essay · Capital Markets Technology

From Network Architect to Trading Network Specialist

Twenty years of infrastructure, three continents, one market that changed everything — and what crossing that frontier taught me about the value of the outsider.

JC
Josimar Caitano
Principal Solutions Architect · NTT DATA · Capital Markets Infrastructure
September 2026 8 min read Career · Market Infrastructure

Electronic trading infrastructure is not a destination most engineers plan for. It is a world that operates in nanoseconds, shapes global liquidity, and keeps capital markets functioning without ceremony. When everything runs correctly, no one notices. When it does not, the consequences arrive before most people can formulate a response.

I did not enter this domain because it was the logical next step. I entered because I have never retreated from unfamiliar territory.

NOTA DO AUTOR — O NOVATO

Ao longo deste texto eu me chamo de “O Novato”. Pode soar estranho vindo de alguém com vinte anos de estrada, mas é exatamente o que eu era ao entrar numa instituição financeira: o cara que entendia de rede como poucos e não fazia ideia de como um banco funcionava por dentro. Levei um tempo para perceber que aquilo não era um defeito no meu currículo. Era a minha maior vantagem.

I

The Advantage of the Outsider

Twenty years building critical infrastructure across American, European, and Asian enterprises left me with something the financial markets did not expect to find in a network architect: the capacity to perceive what those inside the system had long since stopped questioning.

The outsider is not an intruder. The outsider is the professional who arrives without the accumulated bias of institutional familiarity — who still asks the obvious question that no one inside the room dares to raise. In the right environment, that question is worth considerably more than the answer everyone already has.

When I joined a brokerage connected to a stock exchange, I did not know how a bank operated. I did not know how a broker-dealer functioned. I did not know the back office, the pre-trade workflow, the post-trade settlement cycle, the regulatory architecture, or the clearing mechanisms. What I knew with precision was what happened to every packet that crossed that network — and that knowledge was exactly what no one in the room could articulate.

The outsider does not accept “this is how it has always been done” as an answer. That question, asked in the right room, is worth a fortune.

II

The Moment Scale Becomes Real

Every engineer working at the intersection of infrastructure and financial markets has a turning point. A moment when the system ceases to be an abstraction and reveals itself as something alive, consequential, and unforgiving.

For me, that moment arrived inside a brokerage co-located with a stock exchange. There was no drama, no cinematic trading floor atmosphere. Only servers equipped with specialized network interface cards — components priced above three thousand dollars per unit — generating two simultaneous flows: a multicast channel carrying market data, and a TCP order stream traversing a precisely engineered stack of switches, routers, and exchange gateways.

Each packet crossing that infrastructure represented:

  • A capital allocation decision from an institutional fund
  • A dealer adjusting exposure before the market moved
  • An order that required processing within a fraction of a millisecond
  • A real client whose capital depended on absolute precision

Until that point, I had seen multicast carry television signals across carrier networks. In that co-location facility, I understood for the first time that the same protocol can carry something of an entirely different order of magnitude. The network was not supporting the market. The network was the market.

SCALE

Where human time ends and market time begins

Orders of magnitude, drawn on a logarithmic axis. The distance between a human blink and a full trading cycle is not a difference in speed — it is a difference in dimension.

I ran the arithmetic once, out of curiosity. A human eye blinks in roughly one hundred milliseconds. In that single blink, an order leaving one of those servers could reach the matching engine, be filled, and have the confirmation travel back — more than three hundred times over. We were not operating faster than people. We were operating in a dimension where the question of human speed had stopped being relevant.

III

Navigating Complexity Without Surrendering Identity

High-performance trading environments test more than technical competence. They test who you are when the vocabulary surrounding you is not your own — when your credentials belong to a discipline that the room has not yet learned to respect.

There were meetings where my background was assessed before my solution. There were moments of institutional pressure where the trader needed an answer in seconds and the diagnostic was still in progress. The outsider experiences this not as humiliation, but as signal: here is a gap that no one has yet closed.

That realization reoriented everything. I did not abandon the identity I had built across two decades. I expanded the territory it covered.

IV

The Credibility That Is Earned, Not Claimed

In environments where the stakes are measured in milliseconds and basis points, credibility is not declared in a résumé. It is constructed through a sequence of deliverables that others declined to attempt:

  • Understanding and building an international arbitrage environment that allowed the trading team to select optimal execution between Brazil and the United States in real time
  • Presenting to the CTO and business leadership the case for how an HFT/ULL infrastructure could materially impact business outcomes — translating engineering precision into investment thesis
  • Becoming the professional called when the problem had already defeated the available expertise
  • Earning the trust of business stakeholders through consistency, not volume

These contributions do not appear in headlines. They build the foundation that allows you, in time, to sit across from any managing director at any global institution and be received as a peer.

V

What Twenty Years Taught Me

01

The outsider retains an advantage the insider forfeited: the willingness to ask the question everyone else has normalized into silence.

02

Hostile environments are not obstacles. They are maps — they reveal precisely where the territory has not yet been claimed.

03

Executive confidence does not precede the work. It is the residue of having done what others declined.

04

Complexity does not reward bravado. It rewards the professional who remains in the field when others withdraw.

05

Fear is not the adversary. It is the signal that you are standing exactly where growth is possible.

VI

Why I Write This

When I entered the financial markets, I did not know anyone who had crossed from network infrastructure into trading technology and mapped the full terrain. I did not know how long recognition would require. I did not know that the preceding twenty years were precisely the credential that would make me valuable in that environment.

I did not know how a bank functions. I did not know how a brokerage operates. I know now that the full scope extends far beyond network architecture — clearing, settlement, regulatory frameworks, back office, the IPC and RTM networks that carry voice and data between desks, and the submarine cables that decide which continent hears a price first. There remains a great deal to learn.

And it is precisely that awareness — that productive discomfort in the face of what remains unknown — that functions as the structural foundation.

Because the professional who learns to convert that discomfort into forward motion does not stop at the technical room. Does not stop at the co-location facility. Does not stop at the brokerage floor.

That professional arrives at the table where the decisions are made.

I write this for anyone who looks at financial markets technology — or any domain that appears too vast, too specialized, too foreign — and thinks: this is not for me.

CLOSING

You do not need certainty to begin. You need the first step. Your path does not need to be perfect. It only needs to be yours.
